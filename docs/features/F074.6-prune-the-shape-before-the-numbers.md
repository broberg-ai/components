**Source:** fd-sundhed, 2026-08-29, from reading their OWN cleanup job to answer us

## Why this stayed open, and why that is still right

| consumer | prunes? | measured |
|---|---|---|
| xrt81 | no — calls the absence a gap | ~~304 rows in ~10 days, 15 people~~ — **that figure was 2.5× too high; see below** |
| cardmem | never | warned against designing a TTL from an assumption they have one |
| fd-sundhed | yes — 30 days, flat | 42 rows today; go-live **Tue 1 Sep 07:00**, 16,838 users — figures usable after a week |

Three orders of magnitude between the smallest and the largest. A default

## What changed

fd-sundhed made the distinction that unblocks this: **the numbers need September;
the shape does not.** And they handed over two findings made while reading their
own `api/cron/notification-cleanup` to answer our question — measured in their
code, not remembered.

### 1 · A single age cutoff deletes the messages that still had an errand

Their job deletes everything where `created_at` is older than 30 days, **read or
unread**. So an unread notification vanishes on day 31 having never been seen.

That is backwards from what a notification *is*: the unread one is the one still
carrying a task. They found it answering us and said plainly it is probably
wrong.

**Shape decision: two numbers, not one.** Delete read after X, unread after Y.
And the API must not let a caller express "everything older than N" by accident —
if that is what they want, they should have to say it twice.

**Their own framing is sharper than mine, and it is why this is a shape question
at all** (fd-sundhed, correcting my write-up an hour later):

> *our mistake was not that we chose the wrong numbers, it was that we only had
> one. One number cannot express "an unread message still has an errand", so the
> decision was made by an omission instead of by anyone.*

That is the argument for the core handing back a **predicate** rather than
performing a cleanup: the consumer then inherits the decision explicitly, instead
of inheriting whatever our single default happened to imply. They offered it
while stating plainly that their opinion should carry no weight here, because
they are not a consumer — which is the right way to hand over an observation.

### 2 · A retention setting that looks like it works

`notification_auto_delete_days`, per user, **is ignored**. The code uses 30 for
everyone, with a comment saying it can be extended later. The user can set it,
the form saves it, and nothing reads it.

This is the shape this repo has now named six separate times in a week: **a field
that is accepted and does nothing**, indistinguishable from one that works. Their
rule for us is binary and correct — *honour the per-subscriber value, or do not
offer the field.*

## The reason there were two hand-rolled TTLs to find bugs in

Because this package has no prune. Worth writing down rather than treating their
code as a curiosity: the gap is what produced the implementations, and the
defects are what the gap costs.

## Decided here (shape)

- Read and unread expire on **separate clocks**.
- A per-subscriber retention value is **honoured or absent** — never accepted and
  ignored.
- Whatever prunes runs the **same choreography as every other mutation**: write,
  recount through the store's single counting function, announce. A prune that
  deletes counted rows without announcing is F074.5's defect on a schedule.

- **A prune is not per-subject, and that is what makes it hard** (raised by
  xrt81, 2026-09-01, asking what the contract between prune and the count is).
  Every existing mutation is scoped to one subscriber: `mutate()` takes a
  `subjectId`, and `settle(subjectId)` recounts and announces for that one
  subject. A TTL sweep crosses many subjects in one pass, so it cannot simply
  call `mutate()` — there is no single subject to settle.

  The consequence is a **store requirement, not an implementation detail**: the
  prune operation must return **the set of subjectIds it actually touched**, and
  the core settles each of them. A prune that returns only a row count — the
  obvious signature, and the one every ORM makes easiest — *cannot drive the
  announcement at all*, and a core built on it would delete counted rows and
  leave every affected badge stale. That is F074.5's defect again, once per
  sweep, across every subscriber at once.

  Both directions have to be wrong to be caught: settle a subject whose count did
  not change and you send a pointless announcement; miss one whose count did and
  you have a stale badge with nobody told. So the test needs a sweep touching
  **three** subjects where only **two** lose rows, asserting exactly two
  announcements with the right ids — a fixture where the affected set and the
  swept set differ. A sweep where every subject loses a row passes on a core that
  simply announces to everyone.

### 3 · xrt81's rate was not a rate — and a small tenant has the opposite problem

Measured on their production data, 2026-09-06, correcting the figure this doc
carried in the table above:

```
360 rows · 17.0 days · 29 active members

chat_all              217 rows   ALL of them inside 0.4 DAYS
everything else       143 rows   spread over the full 17 days
last 7 days             0 rows
```

**All 217 `chat_all` rows landed on one day** — 13 August, when the club chat was
first used. It is a one-off adoption storm, not traffic.

```
without chat_all   143 / 17 days / 29 members = 0.29 rows per member per day
with it                                         0.73   ← 2.5× too high
```

Their own framing, and it inverts what a TTL is for at this size:

> a TTL of 30 days would today delete our **entire** history without freeing
> anything at all — 360 rows, 0.5 % of what fd-sundhed will produce. **A small
> tenant does not have a volume problem; it has a history problem.**

**Shape decision added: the floor is a COUNT, not only an age.** "Always keep the
newest N per recipient, regardless of age." A pure age cutoff is the only variant
that can empty a list completely, and the consumer it empties is precisely the one
with too little traffic to notice.

That is the same failure as the two-clocks decision one level out: X and Y chosen
on fd-sundhed's 16,838 users would silently empty every small tenant, and nothing
would error.

### 4 · "Touched" is ambiguous on exactly the axis the test measures

xrt81, on the store requirement above: `prune` returning "the subjectIds it
touched" is right, but **touched-rows and changed-count are not the same set.**
A muted row does not contribute to `countUnseen` — that filter is the store's, by
design — so a recipient can lose rows **without the count moving**.

Verified in our own code rather than accepted: `settle()` calls
`onCountChanged` **unconditionally**, after recounting. So a sweep that removes
only muted rows announces to a subject whose badge did not move.

**Their placement argument is the load-bearing part:** let `prune` return the
touched set, and let **`settle` itself** decide whether there is anything to
announce — otherwise the rule exists in two places and the two can drift.

> **Superseded by §5.** The concern is real and the remedy was wrong: a
> muted-only prune is the case that MOST needs announcing, not the one to
> suppress. Left standing rather than deleted, because the reasoning is how the
> decision was reached.

**Where we do not fully agree, stated so the decision is made rather than
inherited:** making `settle` announce only on change would also change the
per-subject mutations, which are shipped and in use by moovyy (0.2.0). Those have
a different contract: a caller who asked for `clear()` is entitled to the count
back, changed or not. So the honest shape is **one function, one explicit
parameter** — `settle(subjectId, { announce: "always" | "on-change" })` — so the
difference between a user action and a sweep is *declared* in one place instead
of implemented twice. That keeps xrt81's property (no drift) without silently
changing an existing consumer.

### 5 · …and their next reply removed the reason for the parameter

xrt81 found two holes in that proposal, and the second one collapses the first.

**Hole one — the signature could not work.** `settle(subjectId)` runs AFTER the
write, so the before-count is already gone; `settle(id, {announce:"on-change"})`
has no way to evaluate its own flag. It would have to be either
`settle(id, {announce:"on-change", was})` or, better, `prune` returns
`Map<subjectId, wasCount>` — prune is the only place that sees both moments, so a
caller cannot forget to capture the first. Their preference, and it is right.

They also asked for the parameter to be **required, with no default**, because
either default is wrong for the other half. Also right, and it is the general
form of this doc's own thesis: *the decision must not be inheritable.*

**Hole two — there are TWO events, not one.** `onCountChanged` carries a number,
but a prune also changes the **list**, and the two separate on the same axis as
mute:

```
sweep removes only MUTED rows  →  count unchanged, LIST SHORTER
"on-change" suppresses the announcement
→ the consumer's list is never refreshed
→ the user is looking at rows that no longer exist
```

Their framing: *what should decide whether to announce is "did anything change
for this recipient", not "did the COUNT change". For a per-subject mutation those
coincide. For a sweep they do not.* And it is worse in a prune than in a mark,
because the rows are not merely mis-highlighted — they are gone.

**So the parameter loses its justification.** Follow it through: `prune` returns
only the subjects whose rows it actually deleted, so **for every subject in that
set, something changed for that recipient — by construction.** The case the
`on-change` mode was invented to suppress (count unchanged, muted-only) is
exactly the case that most needs the announcement.

**Decision, simpler than either of us proposed:** no `announce` parameter, no
`wasCount`, no second count. `prune` returns the touched set; `settle` announces
for each; announcing is unconditional as it is today. The store requirement above
is unchanged — `subjectId[]`, not a map.

A stale list is the defect this package exists to prevent, and we would have been
building it in on purpose.

**But "a redundant refresh is cheap" was MY sentence and it is not the core's to
make.** xrt81 corrected it with their own wiring, measured rather than
remembered:

```ts
onCountChanged: () => {
  /* F079.2 — syncBadge moves in here when the old call is removed. */
}
```

`syncBadge` is not a re-render. It looks up the recipient's push subscriptions
and calls `sendSilent()` — **one web push per device.** So for them a single
announcement costs a network call to Apple or Google per phone.

For a sweep at their size that is 29 members × their devices, from a maintenance
job. At fd-sundhed it is 16,838 users. The conclusion above is unchanged — a
stale list is still worse — but **"cheap" is a property of the CONSUMER'S
wiring, not of this package**, and at least one of our four has wired it to
something that leaves the machine.

**Shape note added, and it does not change the contract:** `onCountChanged` may
be EXPENSIVE, so **prune must be able to run in batches** — not because the core
schedules anything, but so a consumer with expensive wiring has somewhere to
intervene short of writing their own prune. Their line for why it belongs in the
doc rather than in a future issue: *otherwise the first one to get a bill from
Apple is also the first one to find out.*

**Recorded because it nearly went the other way:** we had agreed on a shape, in
writing, and it was wrong. It survived one round because both of us were
answering "how do we avoid a pointless announcement" instead of asking whether a
pointless one costs anything at all.

### 6 · At least one prune test must enter the way a consumer does

xrt81's, from an unrelated hour of their own the same morning: they added a
prefix to a calendar id, the tests were green, and production was wrong — because
the test called the function DIRECTLY while the real caller passed something
else. A green test about a function, read as a claim about the system.

So the prune suite may not consist only of calls to `mutate()` / `prune()` in
isolation. At least one case must arrive through the path a consumer actually
uses.

**And it goes in as an ACCEPTANCE CRITERION, not as a convention here.** That is
xrt81's amendment, and the reason is their own cost:

> the bug was **live for three minutes and went out to every subscriber's
> calendar** — 33 of 33 titles wrong. Five unit tests green. They called the
> function directly with a value the real caller never sends.

What caught it was not a test. It was a *card's* wording — "measured in the live
feed, not only in a test". Their generalisation is the part worth keeping:

> a convention can be satisfied in good faith and still miss. **A criterion
> forces you to say WHERE you measured.**

## Deliberately NOT decided here

- **X and Y.** September. And now with a second axis: **N**, the per-recipient
  count floor, which xrt81's measurement says matters more than X and Y at the
  small end.
- **Whether the core prunes at all**, versus handing the consumer a predicate.
  cardmem never prunes; a core that assumes everyone does fits exactly one app —
  which is the trap the count query already taught this package once. The
  decision goes in this doc with its reason *before* any code.

## Rollout

1. Write the core-prunes-vs-consumer-prunes decision into this doc, with its
   reason. No code before it.
2. Quote fd-sundhed's September figures and xrt81's growth here. Then choose X
   and Y.
3. Build to the shape above, with the announce path proven by a store whose
   count cannot be derived by arithmetic on the pruned ids.
4. Mutation pass: collapsing the two lifetimes, and ignoring the per-subscriber
   value, must each turn a test red.
